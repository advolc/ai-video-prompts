# Pass 5: Body, Status, and Proxemics

Body language is the largest channel the old skill left almost unused. Two people at a table are not mannequins who take turns moving their mouths. They sit, shift, hold things, listen, and make status moves the audience feels before it understands them. This pass replaces v1's "one life-action per scene" cap. Plan macro dialogue geometry with [spatial blocking](../dialogue/spatial-blocking.md) before enriching body detail; a hand gesture is not a replacement for that plan. Body beats are **Tier B**: generous, anchored, readable. Terms are **confirm per model**; test how many simultaneous beats your model can follow.

## 1. Status is a transaction

In every two-person scene, each person is playing **high**, **low**, or **even** status relative to the other, and it can change mid-scene. The Director's Room writes a one-line **status ledger**: who holds status at the start, what shifts it, and where it lands.

Example: *Chen (high) / Wu (low). Wu's news about the shipment drains Chen's patience; Chen answers by stretching out his silence instead of raising his voice, and the status gap widens. At the end Wu tries a small status claim by finishing his own sentence without a stammer, and Chen lets it pass.*

The prompt never says "status". It shows the channels below.

## 2. Status channels

| Channel | 中文 | High status shows as | Low status shows as |
|---|---|---|---|
| Height and elevation | 高度 | Sits deeper and higher; stands while the other sits; the better chair | Perches, sits lower, stands when it is rude to |
| Space | 空间占有 | Takes up room: limbs spread, an arm along the chair back, objects spread across the desk | Contained: knees together, elbows in, things stacked close |
| Orientation and openness | 朝向与开放 | Body angled away, torso open, unguarded center | Body turned toward the other, hands guarding the front |
| Stillness and tempo | 静止与节奏 | Slow, unhurried, long still holds | Quick, frequent small adjustments |
| Eye control | 视线控制 | Looks first, looks longest, looks away last or does not need to | Breaks eye contact first, checks the other's face for permission |
| Touch | 触碰 | Touches objects and space freely; rarely self-touches | Touches self: neck, cuff, hair, hands |
| Response latency | 回应延迟 | Lets silence stand, answers late | Answers fast, fills silences |
| Voice | 声音 | Lower, slower, fewer words, lets sentences end | Higher, faster, trailing, apologetic |
| Ownership | 所属 | Moves through the other's belongings, owns the room | Asks, waits, stays out of bounds |

## 3. Proxemics (distance bands)

Edward Hall's widely used bands: **intimate** about 0–0.45 m, **personal** 0.45–1.2 m, **social** 1.2–3.6 m, **public** beyond that. They vary by culture and situation; treat them as starting values. Use them to place the characters and pick the shot: a threat steps into the personal band; a courtesy stays in the social band; a refusal widens the distance.

## 4. Furniture and geometry

- **Behind a desk:** the person behind owns the room; the visitor sits across or at a lower seat.
- **Across vs. corner (90 degrees):** across is formal or adversarial; a corner angle is cooperative or intimate.
- **Same side:** alliance, or someone being managed.
- **Head of table, seat facing the door:** control and sightline.
- **Deep sofa vs. hard chair:** comfort as power; the deep seat makes the visitor sink and look up.
- **Doorway:** whoever stands in it controls entry and exit.
- **Standing over the seated:** pressure; kneeling, bending, or lowering to eye level softens it.

| Posture | 中文 | Reads as |
|---|---|---|

| Sits on the front third of the chair, palms flat on thighs | 只坐椅子前三分之一，双手平放膝上 | Readiness, deference, nerves |

| Arms crossed, weight on one hip | 双臂交叉，重心偏向一侧 | Withholding, defense |

## 6. Hands and prop business

Prop business is the main way a body "thinks" on screen. It must use **ordinary, setting-consistent props** (Texture Authority: open). Every prop beat has a **function**.

| Business | 中文 | Does |
|---|---|---|
| Lifting a cup, lid scraping, sipping | 端杯、拨盖、啜饮 | Delays the answer; punctuates the other's line; shows leisure |
| Setting a cup down with a small click | 杯子轻磕在桌上 | Closes a point; marks a decision |
| Turning the cup on its saucer | 转杯 | Displaces nerves; stalling |
| Pouring or refilling for someone | 斟茶 | Offers or withholds; controls tempo |
| Pen or brush: writing, capping, tapping | 笔：写、盖帽、敲 | Claims record; signals impatience |
| Papers: squaring, flipping, sliding across | 整理、翻动、推过桌面 | Gives or withholds information |
| Glasses: removing, cleaning, resettling | 摘下、擦拭、戴回眼镜 | Buys time; resets the room |
| Cuff, sleeve, watch | 袖口、衣袖、手表 | Time ownership; tidying under pressure |
| Keys, coin, ring turned in the fingers | 钥匙、硬币、戒指 | Anxious handling; hidden calculation |
| Door, window, curtain | 门、窗、帘 | Controls exit and light |
| Prayer beads (佛珠), jade, fan (折扇) | 佛珠、玉、折扇 | Composure, ritual, a closed fan tapped in the palm like a decision |
| Chess stones, tea ceremony tools | 棋子、茶具 | Ritual strategy; ordered movements |
| Weapon hilt: resting hand, thumb on the guard | 剑柄上放手、拇指压护手 | Quiet threat; readiness |

**Prop state is continuity.** Track it: the cup's level, the lid's position, the cigarette's length, the paper pile. Do not reset it between beats or clips.

## 7. Listening is acting

The listener is half the scene. Write what the listener does while the other speaks:

- **Stillness** that lasts through a long answer.
- **Micro-nods**, a one-time slow nod, or a tilt of the head.
- **A glance at the speaker's hands** or the object they mention.
- **A prop beat on the pivotal word** (the cup rises at "Tuesday").
- **A posture shift at a lie or a weak point** (leaning back, tapping a finger, letting the silence lengthen).
- **Looking away at the weakest sentence**, then back.
- **Mirroring or refusing to mirror** the other's posture.
- **Verbal backchannel** in the speech layer ("mm", "right", "是吗") when it fits the character.

## 8. Dyad templates for seated and formal scenes (situation profile A)

These templates assume furniture and a table. For standing, walking, public, farewell, whispered, and post-exertion scenes, use `dialogue/situation-profiles.md` and section 13 below.

| Dyad | Seating and distance | Hands and props | Gaze and tempo | Typical shift |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Superior and subordinate | Superior far back or behind the desk; subordinate perched, low | Superior: cup, pen, leisure. Subordinate: palms on thighs, cuff touch | Superior: slow, long holds. Subordinate: fast, checking | Subordinate steadies and claims a small point |
| Interrogator and suspect | Across a table, close, or a standing interrogator circling | Interrogator: file, pen, a slid photograph. Suspect: hands hidden or gripping | Interrogator: long silence. Suspect: fills silence | Silence cracks the suspect |
| Mentor and student | Corner angle; mentor slightly elevated | Mentor: ritual (tea, brush). Student: attentive stillness | Calm, measured | Student's question lowers the mentor's guard |
| Rivals | Standing at a social-band distance, shoulders squared | Hands near hilts, glasses, or sleeves | Matching stillness, an unblinking contest | One breaks eye contact or posture |
| Estranged friends or lovers | Wide distance, object between them | Handling a shared object (a letter, a cup) | Eyes avoid, then meet | Distance closes by a half step, then stops |
| Cautious strangers | Public to social band, door within reach | Guarded bags, hands visible | Quick glance, away | Trust shown by a hand placed on the table |

## 9. Example: the tea scene as a beat timeline (profile A, seated formal)

*Director's note (internal): Chen is Wu's superior; Wu reports a customs failure; Chen's patience decays; Wu steadies.*

| # | Beat | Anchor |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Chen sits far back in a worn leather armchair, right ankle over left knee, cup balanced on his knee; Wu perches on the front third of a wooden chair, palms flat on his thighs | Opening geometry |
| 2 | Wu swallows, speaks; his stammer catches on "uh" | His line |
| 3 | Chen says nothing; his thumb turns the cup a quarter-turn on its saucer | Silence as a move |
| 4 | Wu's left hand slides to his cuff and tugs | Self-touch under pressure |
| 5 | At "Tuesday", Chen lifts the cup, the lid ticks once | Prop beat on the pivotal word |
| 6 | Chen sips, eyes on Wu over the rim | Eye control |
| 7 | Chen sets the cup down with a small click, lets the silence run | Closes the point |
| 8 | Chen repeats, quietly and slowly, "Tuesday." | Echo of the key word |
| 9 | Wu's palms stay flat; he takes one slow breath and finishes the sentence without a stammer | Status shift: a small claim |
| 10 | Chen's ankle changes sides; the lamp light shifts on his shoe | A beat that lets him look unbothered |

The Lens turns this into prose (see `examples/v3-extended-examples.md`).

## 10. Period and culture

This vocabulary is a **starting point**, not universal rules. Gestures, seating order, and distances differ by culture and era. For period pieces, check etiquette (traditional Chinese seating hierarchy, sleeve handling, the fist-and-palm salute 抱拳礼, who sits facing the door) against the setting. For modern workplaces, adjust for company culture. When unsure, choose the gesture that fits the established world and flag the assumption outside the copy block.

## 11. Rules (Tier B)

1. **Anchor every beat** to a line, a cue from the other person, a prop, or a breath. Free-floating fidgets are noise.
2. **One primary hand business per person at a time.** Do not sip, write, and gesture with one hand.
3. **No contradictions:** simultaneous gestures must agree with the posture and the speech.
4. **No loops.** A repeated tic (leg bouncing, pen twirling) reads as a glitch. Use it once with a cause, or not at all.
5. **A status shift has a cause** (a line, a silence, a gift, a door). Show the cause.
6. **Props are ordinary and setting-consistent.** Plot-bearing objects are locked; do not add them. Note added props in the one-line added-texture note.
7. **Fights:** during Tier A contact, status shows through footwork, space, stillness, and where weight sits, not through prop business.
8. **Continuity:** posture, prop state, and distance at the end of a clip are the seam into the next.
9. **Do not diagnose.** Describe the behavior; leave the reading to the viewer.

## 12. Life micro-actions (replaces v1's one-per-scene cap)

Scan the present stimulus (cold, heat, sound, someone's words), then the body's adaptation, then the contact detail on an object already in hand. Examples: a cold room and a cup already in hand, so his fingers sit closer to the warm wall of the cup; an evasive answer with a key already in hand, so a fingertip stops on its teeth. They are conditional examples, not a stock list. If the object is not there, do not add it just for the example. A new retrieval, a hand switch, or a slip that changes the scene is a Tier A event, not a micro-action.

## 13. Situation routing

The vocabulary above was first built for seated scenes. Adapt it by situation (`dialogue/situation-profiles.md`); do not import seated business into a standing or moving scene.

| Situation | How status and behavior change |
|---|---|
| Standing | Read who proposes a distance, route or place and how the other responds. Stillness, mobility, standing height and physical capacity do not by themselves establish status. Hands support the designed relationship rather than filling silence |
| Walking | Pace, lead and follow, and alignment carry status. Slowing or stopping is a status move; speeding up is an escape; stepping into the other's path is confrontation |
| Public with onlookers | Status is performed for the audience; volume drops, gestures shrink, and the rupture is a status event when a mask fails |
| High arousal | Status signals compress. Composure becomes the signal of status; losing it is a status loss and can be the turn |
| Exhausted or injured | Preserve support needs, reach and breath limits in the spatial plan. Leaning, sitting or using an aid is a physical state, not automatic loss of authority |
| Intimate or private | Status flattens; distance, touch, and who initiates are the vocabulary |
| Confined space (car, kitchen, elevator) | Check actual routes, seating, visibility and access before selecting gaze, posture or movement. Driving or proximity to the exit alone does not determine relational power; do not invent confinement |
| Moving platform (horse, boat, cart) | Balance and control of the platform are status; who holds the reins, the oar, or the wheel |

Rules: choose the situation first; props must exist in the setting; a cup is one choice, not the default business; status in motion is shown by space and pace before hand business; when the situation changes, the status vocabulary changes with it.
